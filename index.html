It is 1792. Across Europe, governments and navies depend on printed mathematical tables — logarithms, trigonometric values, interest rates — to navigate ships, aim artillery, and manage finance. The problem: every table has been calculated by hand, and human calculators make mistakes. Errors compound silently through the pipeline, reaching the printer uncorrected, and cost lives.

You have been appointed superintendent of a small table-making office in London. Your task is to organise the labour. Following the method pioneered by Gaspard de Prony — and later admired by Charles Babbage — work is divided into three specialised stages: theorists derive the formulas, translators convert them into step-by-step instructions, and computers perform the arithmetic. A fourth stage, checking, is optional — but without it, every error your computers make will be published to subscribers.

You must hire workers, assign them to roles, and run the office for ten weeks. The pipeline moves at the speed of its slowest stage — flood the computing floor with cheap junior computers and you will produce fast, error-riddled tables. Invest in senior computers and checkers and you produce slowly but accurately. Overspend on wages and the treasury runs dry. Cut corners on checking and your reputation collapses.

Every decision involves a trade-off. Errors do not announce themselves — they propagate quietly through the system until a subscriber finds them in print. The only defense is redundancy: a checking stage that catches mistakes before they leave the office.

Survive ten weeks with your reputation above 40 and your treasury solvent to complete the season.

How to Play
  • Hire workers into each stage using the + buttons below.
  • The pipeline throughput equals the slowest stage — find the bottleneck.
  • Errors from each stage multiply together before reaching the checkers.
  • Unchecked tables go straight to print — errors destroy reputation.
  • Click Run Week to simulate one week and read the ledger.
  • Dismiss a worker with the × on their card.
